# Grounded use cases: real workflows, real savings, with proof

These are synthetic personas, but every pain point, workflow step, and dollar
figure below is anchored to a real report, study, or program mechanic, cited at
the end. The goal is not to invent a new workflow. It is to find where an
eligibility step already exists in someone's day, show that it is slow and
knowledge-dependent today, and prove that compressing it saves real money or
real headache.

The test each case has to pass: it must slot into a workflow that already
happens, make it simpler, and the saving must be mechanical (you can show the
math) or measurable (you can run a before/after). Where the saving is only a
plausible story, this doc says so.

---

## Case 1: LTD claimant and the disability insurer (the provably-saves-money one)

**Who:** Priya, 54, off work on long-term disability through her employer's group
plan (insurer: one of Manulife, Sun Life, Canada Life, Desjardins, RBC).

**The workflow today.** Canadian group LTD policies require the claimant to apply
for CPP Disability, and the insurer reduces the LTD payment by the CPP amount it
pays. This is a standard contractual offset: on a $3,000/month LTD benefit, a
$1,000 CPP-D approval drops the insurer's cost to $2,000, and Priya's total stays
the same [1]. Today the insurer's case manager chases the application by letter,
Priya finds the CPP-D forms daunting, and the file drifts for months. Insurers
already pay third-party representation firms to push these applications through,
precisely because the offset is worth real money to them.

**Where Covered fits.** At claim intake, Covered screens Priya for CPP-D, the DTC,
and provincial drug coverage (Trillium or ODB), prepares what it safely can, and
tracks status. It slots into the intake step the case manager already owns.

**Money, with proof.** The CPP-D offset is dollar-for-dollar and contractual [1].
Every approved claim is a direct, calculable reduction in the insurer's LTD
liability. This is the rare case where the saving is arithmetic, not a forecast,
and there is an existing price benchmark: what insurers already pay representation
firms per file.

**Headache removed.** Priya gets one guided path through an application that is
documented as hard, instead of a stack of letters.

**Honest caveat.** Covered does not adjudicate CPP-D; Service Canada does, and it
denies often. The DTC adds gateway value (it unlocks the Canada Disability
Benefit and the RDSP), but as a non-refundable credit it is worth little to a
claimant with no taxable income except as that gateway. See Case 4.

## Case 2: The caregiver who cannot find what exists

**Who:** Marcus, 48, working full time, caring for his mother with dementia.

**The workflow today.** This is the best-documented pain in the space. In a peer-
reviewed study of Ontario caregivers navigating publicly funded home care,
families reported that no one proactively tells them what exists. One caregiver
put it plainly: "If you don't go online and do your research... you have no clue"
[2]. Assessments are done on a good day and miss the daily reality, approve fewer
hours than asked, and use "he lives with family" as a reason to give less [2].
There is no single point of contact who can answer follow-up questions about cost
or future eligibility [2]. Marcus pays out of pocket to fill gaps: Ontario
caregivers average about $758 a month out of their own pockets, and 69% report
being too burnt out to be sure they can keep going [3]. Meanwhile the money he is
entitled to goes unclaimed: DTC take-up is below 40%, and only about a third of
eligible people have an RDSP [5][6].

**Where Covered fits.** One screen at the moment Marcus first reaches out (a
hospital social worker, a 211 call, an EAP line) returns the full eligible set,
the DTC gateway chain, and the federal caregiver credit, each with the real next
step. It replaces weeks of scattered research with one map.

**Money, with proof.** The unclaimed benefits are quantified: documented non-take-
up of the DTC and RDSP means a large share of eligible families are leaving money
on the table [5][6]. The $758/month out-of-pocket figure is the headache in
dollars [3].

**Headache removed.** A single ordered list instead of the documented fumble, and
the DTC gateway surfaced before Marcus spends a year not knowing it exists.

**Honest caveat.** Covered cannot create PSW hours. The supply shortage and the
assessment capping are real and outside its reach [2][4]. It only improves the
benefits-and-funding layer, and it should say so rather than imply it fixes care
access.

## Case 3: The senior who stopped filing and lost GIS

**Who:** Teresa, 77, low income, stopped filing a tax return after her husband
died because "I have no income to report."

**The workflow today.** The Guaranteed Income Supplement is recalculated every
July from the previous year's tax return. If a senior does not file, Service
Canada cannot recalculate it and payments stop [7]. Nobody flags the connection
until the money disappears, and GIS is one of the most under-claimed benefits for
exactly this reason.

**Where Covered fits.** The screen detects the risk from her profile and returns
one instruction: file a return to keep GIS, and in doing so unlock ODB, Trillium,
and CDCP. It fits the same intake conversation as the other cases.

**Money, with proof.** GIS is worth thousands of dollars a year, and the loss
mechanism (stop filing, lose GIS) is documented program behaviour, not a guess
[7]. The saving is the restored benefit.

**Headache removed.** One clear cause-and-effect instruction instead of a mystery
drop in income.

**Honest caveat.** Covered does not file the return. It flags and routes to a free
tax clinic or a filing service; the act still has to happen.

## Case 4: The non-refundable-credit trap (why honesty is the feature)

**Who:** Same population as above: a low-income senior with little or no tax
payable.

**The workflow today.** Generic benefit finders tell this senior "you qualify for
the Disability Tax Credit, worth about $1,500." For someone with no tax payable,
a non-refundable credit is worth close to $0 to them directly. The real value is
elsewhere: it can transfer to a supporting family member who does pay tax, and it
is the gateway to the Canada Disability Benefit and the RDSP. A tool that shows a
cash number here is simply wrong, and the user acts on a figure that will not
appear.

**Where Covered fits.** This is the differentiator from the honesty work already
in the engine. Covered does not print a fake dollar amount. It routes the credit
to the supporting relative where that applies and surfaces the gateway value
(CDB, RDSP) as the real reason to pursue the DTC.

**Money, with proof.** The transfer and gateway mechanics are CRA rules, not
claims [8]. The proof here is negative and still valuable: Covered does not send
people chasing money that is not there, which is what makes the numbers it does
show trustworthy.

**Honest caveat.** This case produces trust, not a headline dollar figure. That is
the point, and it should be framed as accuracy, not savings.

## Case 5: Employer and group benefits (real, but needs a pilot)

**Who:** An HR or EAP team fielding employees who are on disability leave, hitting
drug-coverage caps, or caring for a parent.

**The workflow today.** The employee calls the EAP or HR, who hand them a PDF and
a phone number. Group drug plans sit as second payer behind provincial programs,
so an employee who should be on Trillium or ODB is sometimes charged to the
employer's plan instead. Disability leaves run long partly because CPP-D and
other supports are not set up.

**Where Covered fits.** A navigation layer inside the benefits portal that screens
the employee and coordinates them onto the public programs they qualify for.

**Money, with proof.** Partial. The drug-plan coordination saving (shifting
eligible costs to the correct public first payer) and the CPP-D offset (Case 1)
are mechanical. The absence-reduction and retention story is plausible but
unproven and needs a measured before/after on a real population. This case should
not be sold on the soft number until a pilot produces it.

**Honest caveat.** The clean money here is the same offset and coordination
mechanics as Cases 1 and 3. The employer-specific upside beyond that is a
hypothesis, and should be labelled one.

## What "proof" actually means here

Split the cases by how the saving is established, because a buyer will:

**Mechanically provable (show the math today):**
- CPP-D offset against LTD. Dollar-for-dollar, contractual [1].
- Drug-plan coordination. Public program is first payer; group plan should not be
  charged for a cost Trillium or ODB covers.
- GIS continuation. Documented program rule: file, keep the benefit [7].

**Measurable (needs a before/after, not a forecast):**
- Caregiver time-to-benefits and out-of-pocket reduction.
- Employer absence duration and retention.
- Navigator or case-manager minutes per file.

**Documented-gap, not yet attributed:**
- DTC and RDSP non-take-up are large and real [5][6], but attributing a specific
  recovered dollar to Covered requires tracking approvals end to end.

The honest pitch leads with the mechanical cases, offers to run the measurable
ones as a pilot, and never presents a documented gap as money Covered already
recovered.

## How it fits the real workflow (and makes it simpler)

The common pattern across every case: there is already an intake or assessment
moment where someone, a case manager, a social worker, an EAP agent, a navigator,
is supposed to figure out what the person qualifies for, and does it from memory
and PDFs. Covered does not add a new step. It compresses that existing step into a
two-minute deterministic screen with the forms prepared and the next action
named. Simpler for the worker, faster for the person, and the eligibility logic is
auditable and drift-checked so it does not quietly go stale.

The one thing to resist: bolting on a second workflow. Covered earns its place by
making one existing step shorter, not by asking anyone to adopt a new habit.

## Deep dive: the CPP-D offset, end to end (expands Case 1)

### The exact workflow, insurer side

1. **Off work, STD.** The employee goes on short-term disability, usually up to
   17 to 26 weeks under the group plan.
2. **LTD transition.** At the end of STD the claim moves to long-term disability.
   There is an "own occupation" period (commonly 24 months), then an "any
   occupation" test.
3. **The CPP-D letter.** The policy's coordination-of-benefits clause requires the
   claimant to apply for CPP Disability. The case manager sends the "you must
   apply" letter. Some policies also require appealing every denial [9].
4. **The application.** The claimant applies to Service Canada: the application
   form, a medical report from the treating physician, and work history. The
   test is "severe and prolonged," plus a contributory requirement. ESDC's target
   is 120 calendar days for a regular file (30 days for a grave condition, 5
   business days for terminal), and real waits often run longer [10].
5. **Approval.** The insurer offsets the LTD benefit dollar-for-dollar from the
   approval date. If CPP-D pays a retroactive lump sum covering months the
   claimant already received full LTD, the insurer recovers that overpayment [1].
6. **Denial.** Roughly half of first applications are denied (estimates range from
   about 50% to 65%, no clean official figure) [10]. The policy often forces
   reconsideration (a 90-day window, then 2 to 4 months) and a Social Security
   Tribunal appeal, dragging the file 6 to 18 months [10].
7. **Estimate-and-deduct.** Some insurers estimate the CPP-D amount and deduct it
   whether or not it is received, which pressures the claimant to pursue it [9].

### Where the insurer's money actually leaks

The pain is not only "did they apply." It is three specific leaks:
- **Stalled or missing applications.** Every month without a CPP-D approval, the
  insurer pays the full LTD with no offset. At an average new-beneficiary CPP-D of
  about $1,234 a month, each delayed month is roughly $1,234 of offset not
  captured [10].
- **Poorly prepared applications that get denied.** A denial that then wins on
  appeal delays the offset by a year or more.
- **Time.** The 120-day target is frequently missed, and the clock runs on the
  insurer's full payment the whole time [10].

So the lever is application quality and speed, not just submission.

### Where Covered plugs in

At the exact moment the case manager sends the CPP-D letter, Covered:
- **Pre-screens** the claimant against the CPP-D test, so the case manager is not
  chasing people who will never qualify.
- **Prepares the package:** pre-fills the form, generates the physician-report
  request, assembles work history. Better input raises first-pass approval and
  cuts time-to-offset.
- **Screens adjacent money in the same pass:** the DTC (gateway to CDB and the
  RDSP, and transferable to a supporting relative) and provincial drug coverage
  (Trillium or ODB), which the group drug plan sits behind as second payer.
- **Tracks status** and flags the reconsideration deadline on a denial.

It does not adjudicate. Service Canada decides. Covered improves the quality and
speed of the input, which is exactly where the leaks are.

**Why this is claimant-aligned, not adversarial.** The offset leaves the
claimant's total monthly income unchanged, but they gain a permanent federal
benefit (CPP-D continues and later converts to a retirement pension), the DTC
gateway, and a far easier application. Framing and running it as claimant
assistance is not spin, it is the actual structure, and it is the only safe way to
run it given fair-treatment regulation (FSRA in Ontario).

### What the pilot measures

- **Baseline** (historical cohort or a randomized holdout in one block of
  business): among new LTD claimants, the CPP-D application rate, first-application
  approval rate, median months from LTD onset to CPP-D approval, and the share who
  never apply or stall.
- **Intervention cohort:** Covered at intake.
- **Primary metric, captured offset dollars:** sum over claimants of the CPP-D
  monthly amount times the additional months of offset captured, from both earlier
  approval and incremental approvals. The insurer already records CPP-D approvals
  and offset amounts in each claim file, so attribution is clean and computed from
  their own data.
- **Secondary:** first-pass approval lift, median time-to-approval reduction, DTC
  approvals (gateway value), drug costs shifted to the correct public first payer,
  and a claimant satisfaction or complaint rate as a fair-treatment guardrail.
- **Cost side:** Covered's per-file cost versus the insurer's current spend on
  manual chasing and third-party referrals. ROI is captured offset minus Covered
  cost.
- **Kill criterion:** if the intervention cohort shows no lift in approval rate or
  time-to-approval against baseline, the mechanical saving is not there and you
  stop.

### Honest caveats

- Approval is Service Canada's call; Covered can improve the input, not guarantee
  the outcome.
- The estimate-and-deduct clause means some insurers already book a notional
  offset without an approval, which blunts part of the value. The real value
  concentrates on claimants where estimation is not applied, or where a real
  approval resolves a disputed estimate.
- Fair-treatment risk is real. Run and frame it as claimant-assisting, because it
  is, or it draws regulatory and reputational exposure.

## Case 6: The working caregiver and the credit almost nobody claims

**Who:** Aisha, 45, employed full time, caring for her father who has a lasting
impairment.

**The workflow today.** Aisha fits care around work and pays out of pocket. She
almost certainly does not claim the Canada Caregiver Credit: a 2018 CIBC poll
found 43% of caregivers did not know the credit existed and only 12% had ever
claimed it [11]. The CCC is worth up to $8,773 for an infirm adult dependant in
2026, which at the 14% rate reduces her federal tax by about $1,228 [11]. If her
father is approved for the DTC, the unused portion can transfer to her [8]. If she
cuts her hours by more than 40% to provide care, she may also qualify for EI
caregiving benefits: 55% of earnings up to $729 a week in 2026, as a Family
Caregiver Benefit for Adults (up to 15 weeks) or Compassionate Care (up to 26
weeks) [12]. Most working caregivers do not know any of this exists.

**Where Covered fits.** At the same intake, Covered flags the CCC with its claim
line, the DTC transfer, EI caregiving eligibility, and the Ontario Seniors Care at
Home credit (Case 7), each with the next step.

**Money, with proof.** The CCC is about $1,228 in reduced federal tax, against a
documented awareness gap of 43% unaware and 12% ever claiming, so the unclaimed
base is large and sourced [11]. EI caregiving is weeks of income replacement at a
known rate [12].

**Honest caveat.** The CCC is non-refundable, so it helps only because Aisha is
employed and has tax to offset. This is exactly the case that fails for the
no-income senior in Case 4, which is why the engine must check who has tax payable
before showing a dollar figure. EI caregiving requires a real 40%-plus drop in
hours and 600 insured hours, so it is for caregivers who actually reduce work.

## Case 7: The low-income caregiving household and the refundable credit

**Who:** A lower-income Ontario family caring for a senior at home.

**The workflow today.** The Ontario Seniors Care at Home Tax Credit is refundable:
25% of eligible medical and attendant expenses up to $6,000, for a maximum of
$1,500, reduced by 5% of family net income over $35,000 and gone by about $65,000
[13]. Refundable is the key word. It pays even when no tax is owed, so unlike the
CCC and the DTC it reaches a low-income household directly. It is buried in the
Ontario tax forms and most families never claim it.

**Where Covered fits.** For a household in the right income band, Covered flags the
credit and lists the eligible expenses to keep receipts for.

**Money, with proof.** Up to $1,500 in actual cash, refundable, with the income
phase-out stated [13].

**Honest caveat and the design rule it implies.** This pairs with Case 4. For a
low-income household the engine should prefer the refundable credit, which is real
cash, over the non-refundable ones, which are worth nothing to them, and route any
non-refundable credit to a higher-income supporting relative. Picking the right
credit for the household's tax position is the difference between a useful tool and
a misleading one.
